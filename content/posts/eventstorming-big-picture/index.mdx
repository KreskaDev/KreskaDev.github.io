## 1. Your team writes tickets that nobody understands

Backlog refinement, Wednesday morning. Someone reads out a Jira ticket: *"Add a checkbox 'picked up' to the reservation view"*. Everyone nods. Two weeks later, it turns out the frontend developer thought "picked up" meant "book taken from shelf"; the librarian expected "logged into the system"; the accountant needs it for the fines report. Three interpretations of one phrase — visible only in **production**.

The problem isn't in the tickets. It's that **the team has no shared model** of what actually happens in the system. There's no language that's precise, shared, and verifiable in practice. EventStorming is **the fastest technique I know** to build that model — in one morning, on a wall, without code.

This is the first post in a longer series on Domain-Driven Design and the patterns that orbit it: aggregates, CQRS, sagas, mediators, integration events. They all assume one thing — that you have a domain model that the whole team agrees on. EventStorming is how you get there.

## 2. What EventStorming actually is

EventStorming is **a workshop format for mapping complex business domains**, invented by Alberto Brandolini around 2013. Brandolini describes it like this: *"EventStorming is a flexible workshop format for complex business domain exploration"* (*Introducing EventStorming*, ch. 1). The key insight: **events carry the meaning**. Each orange sticky = one fact that happened in the domain. Time flows left to right. Everything else — commands, aggregates, policies, read models, external systems — hangs around the events.

Three reasons the method works in practice:

1. **Everyone sees the same wall at the same time** — a spatial brain-on-the-wall instead of sequential slides. The domain expert, the developer, the analyst and the support lead are all looking at the same picture.
2. **A sticky note is a low barrier** — the domain expert doesn't have to draw UML. Writing *"Book returned"* on a piece of paper is something a librarian will do. Drawing a class diagram is not.
3. **Conflict becomes visible** — when two people place contradictory events on the timeline, the discussion lands on what actually matters: *who* sees the event, *when* it counts, *what* changes after it.

Frame it carefully: **EventStorming is NOT a framework, NOT UML, and NOT a database schema**. It's a **discovery technique**. The artifact is the conversation; the wall is just the medium.

## 3. Three levels — Big Picture, Process, Design

Brandolini distinguishes three levels of EventStorming. They are different workshops with different goals:

| Level | Goal | What you use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **Big Picture** | Map the whole domain — events end-to-end across the business | Onboarding, finding areas of interest, scoping bounded contexts |
| **Process Modeling** | Detail one specific process — commands, policies, sagas | Identifying aggregate boundaries, naming processes |
| **Design Level** | Specify a single aggregate — invariants, decision tables | Going into code, designing the type system |

This post covers **Big Picture** only. Process Modeling and Design Level get their own posts later in this series (see the v6 roadmap).

A useful mental frame: **Big Picture doesn't produce code**. It produces a **ubiquitous language** and a **list of questions for further exploration**. If you leave a Big Picture workshop with code on your mind, you skipped a step.

## 4. Big Picture in action — the Library domain

The running example for the rest of this post is a **public library**. Members borrow books; reservations form a queue; loans can be extended; overdue returns generate fines; lost books trigger a replacement policy. Everyday enough that you can jump in without backstory, rich enough to host every sticky color we need.

We'll build the wall progressively. Each widget below is a snapshot of "what the wall looks like after this round of the workshop".

### 4.1 Round one — only events

First pass. The facilitator asks: *"what happens at the library, in time order?"*. The room writes orange stickies and arranges them left to right.

Two things to notice. First, the events are **in past tense** ("Member registered", not "Register member"). Each event = a **fact** that already happened. Second, instead of seven hours of debate about *"how should this work"*, the team has produced seven sticky notes saying *"what happens when it works"*. That asymmetry is the whole trick.

This first wall is also a great place to spot **missing events**. Did we forget the "Book added to catalog" event? Did we forget "Membership expired"? The wall is loud about gaps, because the timeline visibly skips.

### 4.2 Round two — adding commands

Each event has an "earlier moment" — someone's **command** that caused it. The grammar is fixed by Brandolini: command = intent (imperative mood), event = fact (past tense). Blue stickies above the orange line:

Now look at the last event: **"Late fee charged"**. There's no command for it. Why? Because it's a **policy-driven event** — the system itself issues the fee in response to "Book returned after due date". That single missing blue sticky is the first clue that *"there's something more here than a user-driven flow"*. We'll come back to it in round four (hot-spots) and again in the complete picture.

### 4.3 Round three — actors

Who triggers the commands? EventStorming workshop convention here is **one sticky per role**, not per command. The role is shared across many commands in the mental model. For this slice we have two:

**Two roles**, not seven duplicates of "Member". The Member dominates the user-facing flow; the Librarian is "registrar + edge-case handler". And here's the first **open question** for the domain expert: *"Pick up book — does the member do it on their own, or does the librarian have to confirm?"* Both answers are valid; they imply different auth models. Mental hint: **roles map to your security boundaries**. Member ≠ Librarian at the authentication layer; the wall already tells you that.

> **Convention note:** in a physical workshop a facilitator might place five physical Member stickies, one above each command, so the reader-eye sees "the same actor sits above five commands". In a static visualization grouped by type, duplicates would just look chaotic. The widget renders one sticky per role; prose explains the mental model.

### 4.4 Round four — hot-spots

A red sticky = **a problem or an open question**. This is where EventStorming pays off the most, because it makes invisible uncertainty visible and ownable:

    { type: 'hotspot', label: 'What if the member refuses to pay?',                    x: 3, y: 1 },

These four red stickies are **four sub-processes** nobody had consciously modeled until now. Each one will spawn either a policy (*"reservations form a FIFO queue"*), a timeout-based event (*"Reservation expired"*), a manual flow (*"Damaged book intake"*), or an escalation (*"Account suspension after N unpaid fees"*).

EventStorming **doesn't answer the hot-spots**. EventStorming **shows that you have to ask them** — and points the team at exactly who can answer. Brandolini: *"Hot spots are gold. They mark the boundary between what we know and what we should ask the domain expert"* (*Introducing EventStorming*, ch. 5).

The final wall sketches everything: events + commands + actors + hot-spots + aggregates (yellow large) + policies (purple) + read models (green) + external systems (pink). At the end of Big Picture we have:

- **3 aggregates** (Reservation, Loan, Member Fines) — early hints for aggregate boundaries; Process Modeling will confirm or refine
- **2 policies** (notify on reservation, charge on overdue) — candidates for Sagas / Process Managers
- **1 external system** (Email Service) — the boundary with the out-of-context world; this is where an Anti-Corruption Layer will live
- **2 read models** (active loans UI, overdue list admin) — query side, separated from the command side (CQRS preview, a flagship topic later in this series)
- **1 hot-spot** in the middle of the policy flow ("Email Service down") — arguably the most critical sticky on the wall, because it's an *integration concern*, not a domain concern, and those tend to bite hardest in production

## 5. The sticky color legend

Each color on the wall encodes one concept. Here's the full vocabulary in one place — useful as a quick reference while reading the rest of this series.

  caption="The eight sticky types used across this series."

    { type: 'readmodel', label: 'Read Model',       x: 2, y: 1 },
    { type: 'external',  label: 'External System',  x: 0, y: 2 },
    { type: 'hotspot',   label: 'Hot Spot',         x: 1, y: 2 },
  ]}
/>

| Sticky | What it represents | Grammar | Code-side analogue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Domain Event** (orange) | A fact that happened in the domain | Past tense — *"Member registered"* | `IDomainEvent` published by an aggregate |
| **Command** (blue) | An intent to change state | Imperative — *"Register member"* | Handler input / method call |
| **Actor** (light yellow) | A role that triggers commands | Noun — *"Member"*, *"Librarian"* | Security principal / auth context |
| **Aggregate** (large yellow) | A consistency boundary that protects invariants | Noun phrase — *"Reservation"* | Aggregate Root class |
| **Policy** (lilac / purple) | A rule that reacts to events and issues commands | *"Whenever X, do Y"* | Saga / Process Manager |
| **Read Model** (green) | A view projection optimized for one query | Noun phrase — *"My active loans"* | CQRS query side / projection table |
| **External System** (pink) | Something outside the bounded context | Noun — *"Email Service"* | Anti-Corruption Layer boundary |
| **Hot Spot** (red) | A problem or open question | Question — *"What if...?"* | TODO + domain-expert ticket |

When you scan a wall **left to right**, you're following time. When you scan **top to bottom**, you're following causation: actor invokes command, command produces event, event triggers policy, policy issues another command.

## 6. What you actually get after Big Picture

Not code. Not a database schema. Not a microservices design. You get:

1. **Ubiquitous language** — "Reservation placed" is now a term **everyone** uses. The frontend dev speaks the same way as the librarian and the accountant. This is Evans' original DDD insight, but EventStorming gets you there in a morning rather than over weeks of glossary maintenance.
2. **Initial aggregate boundaries** — yellow large stickies are **candidates** (Reservation, Loan, Member Fines), not a verdict. Process Modeling confirms or corrects them.
3. **A list of open questions** — red stickies are the **agenda** for the domain expert for the next week. They're also, in my experience, the best estimate-confidence indicator a team can produce.
4. **A list of integration points** — pink stickies (External Systems) show where an Anti-Corruption Layer will be needed. The wall makes integration boundaries visible without you having to argue about them.

Brandolini repeats: *"Big Picture is not a deliverable. It's a conversation made visible"* (ch. 4). The wall lasts a few hours; the language lasts the lifetime of the system.

## 7. Anti-patterns — how teams ruin EventStorming

Every methodology post needs a "when this goes wrong" section. Brandolini's list, lightly paraphrased:

1. **"Workshop with PowerPoint slides"** — kills the workshop. Without a wall + stickies + markers, the spatial-thinking magic disappears. A flipchart is fine; a Miro board is fine *as a recording medium*; a deck of slides is not a workshop.
2. **"Tech-led modeling"** — developers write events as `UserDtoCreated` or `BookEntityPersisted`. Events are **domain language**, not class names. "Member registered" beats "MemberAggregateInitialized" every time.
3. **"Skip hot-spots, go straight to design"** — the most common failure. Hot-spots are exactly **where knowledge is missing**. Skipping them just defers the bug to production six months later.
4. **"Tools instead of stickies"** — Miro and Lucidchart are great **after** a workshop, terrible **inside** one. Tools slow the conversation down (mouse, keyboard, layout decisions); a sticky note is the lowest possible barrier to participation.
5. **"Big Picture as a one-shot"** — Big Picture **iterates**. Every month or quarter you add new events as the product roadmap shifts. It's a **living artifact**, not a milestone deliverable.
